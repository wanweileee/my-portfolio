## The brief

A seven-person capstone project for Delta Electronics, designing a collapsible single-user work pod for compact urban homes. The brief recognised that hybrid work has settled into permanence, Singapore homes are getting smaller, and conventional office pods are fixed structures that demand permanent space.

The team designed a pod that opens to a full ergonomic workspace and collapses to roughly the footprint of a vending machine. I was responsible for the mobile application, the layer that turns the pod from a piece of furniture into a connected workspace.

## What the team built

![BOB the Pod deployed at full 1:1 scale, fabricated by the capstone team](/projects/bob1.jpg)

A two-part wooden pod connected by telescopic rails, with acoustic curtains forming the enclosure when deployed. Inside, the seat and table are height-adjustable via linear actuators, and lighting and ventilation are controllable through both physical buttons and the mobile app.

The pod was fabricated at full 1:1 scale, validated through an acoustic test showing 40 dB attenuation in mid- and high-frequency ranges, and received positive feedback from our industry mentor at Delta Electronics.

## Behind the build

A capstone in furniture-scale fabrication means the build itself is most of the project. The acoustic curtains had to be sized, hung, and tested; the rails had to slide cleanly under load; every joint was a small experiment. The team learned each step on the job.

## What I built: the mobile application

A React Native app shipped in two editions, each tuned to a different context of use. The architectural decision to split the app was the most consequential product call I made on the project: the same pod has fundamentally different social dynamics at home (one user surrounded by family) versus in an office (many users sharing the resource), and trying to serve both contexts in a single interface would have produced something compromised in both.

**Office edition.** Centred on booking. Users can browse pod availability by timeslot, reserve a pod, and check in when their session begins. The booking flow handles conflict prevention server-side against a PostgreSQL backend, and users can view both today's and upcoming reservations from the home screen.

**Home edition.** Centred on the *Knock* feature, which I'm proudest of as a design call. By tapping on your colleague's name in the mobile app, it triggers a brief orange LED pulse inside the pod and sends a quick-reply notification, letting the user inside acknowledge with a one-tap response ("5 min," "15 min," "I'm free") without opening their laptop or breaking focus. The feature came out of recognising that the real cost of working from home isn't workspace. It's the friction of being interrupted versus the guilt of being unreachable. Knock collapses that to a one-tap interaction.

**Shared across both editions.** Real-time control over lighting (brightness, colour temperature, three presets for meeting, reading, and relaxation), fan speed, and ventilation, all wired through the pod's IoT pipeline. A dashboard surfaces current settings and a "Smart Suggestion" panel that recommends configurations based on the active mode. An events tab handles team birthdays and activities for the office edition. Profile settings let users set a preferred default mode that the pod applies automatically when they check in.

## How the app talks to the pod

The app integrates with the pod hardware through an AWS-hosted backend, with commands flowing through AWS IoT Core's MQTT publish/subscribe layer down to the pod's ESP32 microcontroller.

From the app side, this meant handling optimistic UI updates carefully: a user expects an LED to respond instantly when they drag a brightness slider, but the round-trip through cloud and MQTT introduces real latency. Getting that feel right took more iteration than the rest of the app combined.

## Reflection

The bond I formed with my teammates while building our physical capstone pod is something I will always remember. Working past midnight in the school fabrication lab was a unique experience that I likely won't have again. Our design process required numerous iterations. Early on, our mentor pointed out that our initial idea was too generic to compete with existing meeting pods. This critique drove us to explore speculative design, which became an enlightening experience that taught me how to think critically about future possibilities.
The lessons learnt here are beyond the technical skills I gain from building the application which I will never forget.
